Adaptive Line Spacing and the Restoration of Reading Stamina in Dense Digital Prose

Marta E. Lindqvist, Idris A. Okonjo, and Petra Halvorsen
Department of Human–Computer Interaction, Northgate University, Northgate, NG1 4QT, United Kingdom
Reading Sciences Laboratory, University of the Coastal Provinces, Harrow Bay, HC2 8LP, United Kingdom
Correspondence to M. E. Lindqvist (m.lindqvist@northgate.example.ac.uk). Received 14 January 2024; accepted 2 June 2024; published 19 July 2024.

Abstract

Long-form reading on screens has become the default mode of intellectual work, yet the typography of the web remains optimised for scanning rather than for sustained attention. Editorial systems inherited from print assume a fixed column, a known paper size, and a reader who has already committed to the text. Browser rendering, by contrast, negotiates an unpredictable viewport, a cascade of competing style declarations, and a layout engine that treats paragraph spacing as one property among many. The result is a class of pages that are technically legible and practically exhausting: dense blocks of text pressed together at line heights well below one and a half, margins measured in single pixels, and almost no rhythm to guide the eye back to the next line. This paper reports a controlled study of what happens when that rhythm is restored.

Keywords: reading stamina, line spacing, paragraph rhythm, extractive reading views, typographic adaptation.

1. Introduction

Readers who work with long documents on screen describe a fatigue that is difficult to attribute to any single defect of the page. The type is large enough, the contrast is adequate, and the words themselves are familiar, yet attention decays faster than it does on paper. Our interviews with forty-two postgraduate researchers and technical professionals produced a consistent account of this experience: the text is not unreadable, it is uninviting, and the effort of remaining in it accumulates over a working session. Participants repeatedly compared the feeling to reading a document whose paragraphs had been compressed by a faulty photocopier, an analogy we found unexpectedly precise once we began measuring the layouts they were describing.

The measurements confirmed the analogy. Across a corpus of three hundred and eighteen web articles, the median body line height was 1.42, the median gap between adjacent paragraphs was four pixels, and the median measure was ninety-one characters per line. Print conventions place those values nearer to 1.5, one full line of space, and sixty to seventy-five characters respectively. The discrepancy is not the product of a deliberate design philosophy. It is the residue of framework defaults, of design tokens inherited from dashboard interfaces, and of a widespread assumption that paragraphs on the web are separated by a blank line in the source rather than by visible space on the screen.

1.1 Motivation

Two observations motivate the present work. The first is that paragraph boundaries carry meaning. When a writer breaks a paragraph, the break signals a change of topic, a shift in argumentative stance, or the introduction of a new example, and a reader who cannot see that boundary must reconstruct it from syntax. The second is that restoration is cheap. Unlike improvements in typographic craft that require an author to rewrite, re-space, or re-typeset a document, the interventions studied here operate on an already-rendered page without touching the underlying markup. A reading system can adjust spacing, line height, and measure at the moment of display, and can do so without the cooperation of the site that produced the text.

1.2 Prior work

The typographic literature offers strong evidence that line spacing influences comprehension, but most of that evidence was gathered on paper or on short passages presented under laboratory conditions. Studies of reading speed on screens generally find that increased leading slows immediate reading while improving recall after a delay, a trade-off that is rarely examined over sessions long enough for fatigue to matter. Work on extraction and readability takes the opposite approach, concentrating on the removal of navigation, advertising, and comment markup, and treating the typography of the extracted article as a downstream concern. Our contribution sits between these traditions: we assume that the text has already been found, and we ask how it should then be presented.

1.3 Contributions

We make three contributions. First, we report a controlled comparison of four spacing policies applied to the same three hundred and eighteen articles, with reading stamina operationalised as the number of minutes a participant continues reading before choosing to stop. Second, we describe a lightweight classification procedure that reconstructs heading structure from computed font sizes and weights alone, and we show that it recovers the intended structure on pages whose markup contains no heading elements at all. Third, we release the measurement instrument and the corpus annotations so that the experiment can be repeated on other collections of pages.

1.3.1 Scope and non-goals

Three boundaries should be stated plainly. We do not attempt to judge the quality of the prose we present, and we make no claim that a well-spaced bad argument becomes a good one. We do not address accessibility requirements beyond the preservation of the document order and the semantic roles that were already present in the source. Finally, we do not study the effects of colour, contrast, or theme inversion, not because those variables are unimportant, but because they interact with ambient light in ways our laboratory could not hold constant across sessions.

2. Method

The study used a within-subjects design. Each participant read six articles from the corpus, one under each of four spacing policies plus two control conditions, with the order of conditions counterbalanced across participants using a Latin square. Sessions were separated by at least forty-eight hours and were scheduled at the same hour of the day for each participant. Reading took place on identical laptops with the display brightness fixed, the operating system set to a light appearance, and notifications suppressed. Participants were told that they could stop at any time and that stopping was a normal outcome of the task rather than a failure of effort.

2.1 Corpus construction

Candidate pages were collected from public archives of long-form journalism, technical documentation, and open-access scholarship. To qualify, a page had to contain at least fifteen hundred words of continuous prose inside a single content region, no more than a quarter of that region had to consist of link text, and the page had to render without scripts beyond those required to deliver the initial markup. After filtering and manual review, three hundred and eighteen pages remained. Paragraph boundaries were annotated by two coders working independently, and disagreements were resolved by inspection of the rendered page rather than of the source, since the two frequently diverged.

2.2 Measurement instrument

Stamina was measured by time to voluntary stop, supplemented by a probe that appeared after every eight minutes of continuous reading and asked whether the participant wished to continue, switch articles, or end the session. The probe was deliberately unobtrusive: it occupied a fixed strip at the bottom of the viewport, required a single keystroke, and disappeared immediately. We also recorded scroll reversals, defined as upward scroll movements exceeding one paragraph in height, as a behavioural proxy for lost place in the text. Comprehension was assessed after each session with six short inference questions drawn from the passage.

2.3 Procedure

Each session began with a two-minute calibration passage that was excluded from analysis. Participants then read the assigned article under the assigned policy, answered the probe when it appeared, and completed the inference questions at the end of the session. The policies were implemented in a browser extension that rewrote computed styles at display time. Crucially, the extension never modified the underlying document: it rendered the extracted text into an overlay that was attached to the page and removed on exit, leaving the original markup byte-identical, which allowed us to verify that no condition had altered the source and that residual effects could not be attributed to persistent page changes.

3. Results

Median time to voluntary stop rose from nineteen minutes in the baseline condition to thirty-one minutes under the policy that combined a line height of 1.6 with a paragraph gap of one and a half lines. The effect appeared in every subgroup we examined and was largest for participants who reported the most prior fatigue. Reading speed fell by roughly seven percent, a cost that was more than offset by the additional minutes of engagement, and comprehension scores were stable within the noise of the instrument. Reversals dropped by a third, suggesting that readers lost their place less often when paragraph boundaries were visible as space rather than inferred from content.

3.1 Aggregate effects

Table-free reporting is deliberate here, because the aggregate hides the shape of the response. Gains were strongly non-linear in spacing: increasing the gap from four to twelve pixels produced most of the benefit, while increasing it beyond twenty-four pixels produced no further improvement and, in three participants, visible irritation. Line height behaved similarly, with the useful range lying between 1.5 and 1.75 and a sharp decline in preference above 1.9. The practical implication is that the intervention is not a matter of adding space without limit. It is a matter of crossing a threshold below which paragraphs do not read as separate units at all.

3.2 Subgroup variation

Participants who read primarily for extraction, such as developers consulting reference material, gained least from the spacing policies and reported that generous spacing forced additional scrolling. Participants who read linearly, including the humanities researchers in our sample, gained most. This split has a direct bearing on design: a reading system that imposes a single spacing policy on every reader and every page will serve one of these groups well and the other poorly, and the difference is not a matter of taste but of the task the reader is performing. Settings that persist per site, rather than globally, appear to strike a workable balance.

3.3 Qualitative reports

Free-text responses were coded into four themes: recoverability of place, perceived speed, perceived effort, and trust in the tool. The first theme dominated. Participants described the spaced view as one in which they could always find the line they had just read, and several volunteered that this alone accounted for their willingness to continue. The second theme ran in the opposite direction to the measured data: many participants believed they were reading faster when they were in fact reading more slowly, a discrepancy that has been documented elsewhere and that complicates any interface that exposes a speed readout. The remaining themes are illustrated by the following comment, which we quote in full because it captures the tone of the corpus.

I did not realise how much of my attention was going into working out where one idea stopped and the next one started until that work was done for me. The words did not change at all, and yet I stopped checking the clock every few minutes, which is not something I can usually say about reading a long report on a laptop screen in the middle of an afternoon.

4. Discussion

The results support a simple account of the mechanism. Spacing does not make text more interesting, and it does not reduce the cognitive cost of comprehending an argument. What it does is remove a recurring, low-level decision about where the current unit of meaning ends. That decision is cheap on its own, but it recurs at every paragraph boundary, and on a dense page those boundaries arrive every hundred words or so. Removing a small cost that repeats two hundred times in a session produces a benefit that looks disproportionate when measured at the level of the session, which is precisely the level at which readers experience fatigue and decide to stop.

4.1 Limitations

Four limitations qualify these findings. The corpus over-represents English-language pages from institutions with reliable bandwidth, and the spacing thresholds we report may not transfer to scripts whose line-breaking rules differ. The sample was small and self-selected, and participants who volunteered for a study about reading fatigue may have been unusually sensitive to it. Time to voluntary stop is a behavioural measure with an obvious demand characteristic, since participants knew that stopping was permitted and were aware that the study concerned persistence. Finally, the six-article session length is short relative to the multi-hour reading sessions that motivated the work, and we cannot say whether the benefits persist, plateau, or reverse over a full working day.

4.2 Implications for practice

For authors of reading systems, three recommendations follow. Preserve the source document exactly, because readers who cannot return to the original page lose the ability to verify what they read and to follow links that the extraction dropped. Derive structure rather than trusting markup, because a substantial fraction of pages express hierarchy through styling alone and a system that only recognises heading elements will flatten them. Finally, make the spacing policy visible and adjustable, since the subgroup differences we observed are large enough that a fixed default will be wrong for a meaningful share of readers, and wrong in a way that those readers can identify immediately but cannot correct.

5. Conclusion

Dense digital prose is not an inevitable consequence of reading on screens. It is a default, produced by tooling that never had long-form reading as a goal, and it can be undone at display time without rewriting a single document. The experiment reported here indicates that a modest increase in line spacing and paragraph separation, applied consistently to an extracted article, roughly halves the rate at which readers abandon a text, at a small cost in raw reading speed and no measurable cost in comprehension. The intervention is cheap, reversible, and compatible with the pages as they are published. We regard the remaining question not as whether spacing helps, but as how a reading system should decide how much of it a given reader, on a given page, actually wants.

// Stamina analysis: median minutes to voluntary stop per policy.
// Deterministic, offline, no dependencies.
const policies = [
  { id: 'baseline',  lineHeight: 1.42, gapPx: 4  },
  { id: 'moderate',  lineHeight: 1.50, gapPx: 12 },
  { id: 'generous',  lineHeight: 1.60, gapPx: 24 },
  { id: 'excessive', lineHeight: 2.00, gapPx: 40 },
];

function medianMinutes(sessions, policyId) {
  const rows = sessions
    .filter((s) => s.policy === policyId && s.calibration !== true)
    .map((s) => s.minutes)
    .sort((a, b) => a - b);
  if (rows.length === 0) return null;
  const mid = rows.length >> 1;
  return rows.length % 2 === 1
    ? rows[mid]
    : (rows[mid - 1] + rows[mid]) / 2;
}

// Reported values: 19, 24, 31, 26 median minutes respectively.
for (const p of policies) {
  console.log(p.id, medianMinutes(sessions, p.id));
}
